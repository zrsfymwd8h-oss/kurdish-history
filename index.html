Who are the Kurds?

The Kurds are one of the largest ethnic groups in the Middle East without an official state. They primarily live across four countries: Iraq, Iran, Turkey, and Syria. Their population is estimated to be between 30–40 million people.

Ancient Kurdish History

The origins of the Kurdish people are linked to ancient Indo-European tribes living in the Zagros Mountains. Historical references to Kurdish ancestors appear in early Persian and Assyrian sources. Over centuries, Kurdish identity developed in the mountainous regions that provided natural protection and independence.

Modern Kurdish History

During the Ottoman and Persian empires, Kurdish regions were divided between major powers. After World War I and the fall of the Ottoman Empire, Kurdish hopes for independence increased but were not fulfilled. The Treaty of Sèvres (1920) briefly mentioned Kurdish autonomy, but it was later replaced by the Treaty of Lausanne (1923), which removed those promises.

Kurdish Political Movements

Republic of Mahabad (1946)

One of the most important moments in Kurdish history was the creation of the Republic of Mahabad in Iran, led by Qazi Muhammad. It lasted less than a year but became a symbol of Kurdish independence movements.

Barzani Movement

In Iraq, Kurdish movements led by Mustafa Barzani played a key role in fighting for autonomy and rights. These movements later influenced the formation of the Kurdistan Regional Government.

Modern Autonomy

Today, the Kurdistan Region in Iraq has its own parliament and government, representing the most developed form of Kurdish self-rule.

Kurdish Culture

Kurdish culture is rich and diverse, shaped by centuries of tradition and mountain life.

  • Language: Kurmanji, Sorani, and other dialects
  • Music: Traditional instruments like daf and tembûr
  • Dance: Govend (traditional circle dance)
  • Clothing: Colorful traditional outfits with cultural meaning
  • Newroz: Kurdish New Year celebration on March 21
